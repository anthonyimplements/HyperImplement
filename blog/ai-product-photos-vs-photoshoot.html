The short answer: it depends on the job each image does

"AI product photos vs a photoshoot" sounds like an either/or question. It isn't, once you look at what each image on your store is actually for.

Some images have to prove the product is real. That's the photo a shopper zooms into to check the stitching, the color, the size. Other images have to stop someone scrolling and make them feel something. That's your lifestyle shot, your ad, your social post.

So here's my rule:

  • Proof images start from real pixels of the real product.
  • Feeling images are where AI pays for itself.

When I shop for something like a patio set, I want to know exactly what shows up at my door. But I also want to picture it in my backyard at golden hour with a drink on the armrest. Those are two different jobs, and they don't need the same tool.

What I'd use for each image on your store

Here's the table I work from. "Real" means a real photo of your product, even a phone photo you cleaned up. "AI" means generated from that real photo plus a brand kit.

If you sell on Amazon, Etsy or another marketplace, check that platform's current image policy before you swap your main image. Those rules change, and they're stricter about the main photo than about the rest.

Where a real photoshoot still wins

I'm bullish on AI, but there are spots where I'd tell a brand to keep the camera:

  • Fine detail people inspect. Fabric texture, stitching, small print on a label. AI likes to "improve" these, and then your photo doesn't match the box.
  • Reflective and see-through products. Glass, chrome, jewelry. These still take reruns to look right, and sometimes they never do.
  • Exact scale and fit. Clothing on a body, furniture in a room where the size matters.
  • Your real team or real customers. If the image says "this is us" or "this is a customer," it has to be real. An AI person shown as a real customer is a fake review, and the FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465) is clear about that.

A simple test: if a shopper would feel lied to when the box arrives, that image needs real pixels.

The hybrid plan I'd start with

You don't need a big shoot to start. You need one good real photo per product.

  1. Take one clean photo of the real product. A phone is fine. Even light, the whole product in frame, no clutter. This is the photo AI will copy from, so take your time on it.
  2. Clean it onto a white background. This becomes your main image and your reference for everything else.
  3. Build a quick brand kit. Logo, colors, fonts and a sample headline. Without it, the model guesses at your brand and usually guesses wrong.
  4. Generate the "feeling" images from that reference. Lifestyle scenes first, then ad versions. In Higgsfield I use GPT Image 2 for stills and Seedance 2.5 when I want video.
  5. Tell the model what not to do. This is the step most people skip. Too often we only tell AI what we want.
  6. Check every output against the real product. Color, label, shape, number of parts. If anything changed, rerun it or toss it.
  7. Only book a shoot for the gaps. Fine detail, scale shots, real people. That's a much smaller, cheaper shoot than "photograph everything."

Here's a lifestyle prompt you can adapt. Attach your clean white-background photo and your brand kit as references.

Place the attached product in a [backyard patio at golden hour, Midwest suburb].
The product must match the reference photo exactly: same shape, color, materials,
and number of pieces. Natural light coming from the left, soft shadows matching
that direction. Shot at eye level, like a real lifestyle photo for an online store.
Leave clean space on the right for a headline.

Negative: do not change the product's color, shape, or materials. No extra
cushions, pieces, or props attached to the product. No text, logos, or
watermarks except the brand logo from the kit. No warped edges, no floating
objects, no distorted hands or faces.

Mistakes I see brands make with AI product photos

  • Starting from a bad photo. AI copies what you give it. A blurry or cluttered reference gives you a blurry, cluttered product.
  • Trusting the first output. Expect to rerun a couple and tweak each prompt. That's normal, not a sign the tool is broken.
  • Letting AI touch the main image. Clean it up, sure. But don't let it redesign your product.
  • Using AI people as "customers." Use them as presenters or demos, and say it's AI where it matters.
  • Making one image and stopping. The real win is volume. If you only need one photo, a single afternoon with a camera might be enough.

Questions people ask about AI product photos

How much does a product shoot cost compared to AI?

It depends on the photographer, the number of products and whether you need a location or models, so get real quotes for your products. For comparison, the AI ads I made for a golf cart company cost me probably under $10 each, versus thousands for a crew and 360-degree camera rigs.

Can I use only AI product photos?

You can, but I wouldn't for your main image or your detail shots. Those need to show exactly what ships. Start from at least one real photo of each product, then let AI handle lifestyle scenes, ads and seasonal creative built from that photo.

What's the best AI for product photos?

The model matters less than the inputs. I use Higgsfield with GPT Image 2 for stills and Seedance 2.5 for video, but any tool does better with a clean reference photo, a brand kit and a prompt that says what not to change.

Will shoppers know the photos are AI?

Good ones are hard to spot, but that's not the bar to aim for. The bar is: does the photo match what arrives? If it does, you're fine. If an AI person is presented as a real customer, that crosses a line, no matter how real it looks.

The bottom line

Shoot what has to be true. Generate what has to be felt.
