How to Make UGC Ads With AI (Without Faking a Review)

No creator, no camera, no shipping samples. Here's how I make AI UGC-style ads that show off a real product, and the one line I don't let a script cross.

What AI UGC is (and what it isn't)

UGC stands for user-generated content. It's the selfie-style video you see in your feed: a person holding the product, talking to the camera like a friend would.

AI UGC is that same format, made with AI instead of a hired creator. The person, the voice and the scene are generated. The product should not be. It should come from a real photo of what you actually ship.

So here's how I think about it. An AI UGC ad is a product demo with an AI presenter. It is not a customer review. That one distinction drives every step below.

Step 1: Research what already works in your niche

Before I make anything, I go to the Meta Ad Library (opens in a new tab) and search the brands in my client's niche. I filter to active ads and look for the UGC-style ones.

The thing I pay the most attention to is how long an ad has been running. A brand doesn't keep paying for an ad that isn't working, so a UGC ad that's been live for months tells you a lot.

For each long-running ad, write down three things:

  1. The hook. The first line or first image that stops the scroll.
  2. The product moment. When the product shows up and what it's doing.
  3. The one feature they lead with. Not five features. One.

Then you remake that structure with your own product and your own spin. You're borrowing the shape of a winning ad, not the ad itself.

Step 2: Write a script around one feature

Most AI UGC fails at the script, not the video. People try to cram the whole product page into 15 seconds.

Pick the one feature a buyer actually cares about. For the patio brand, one of them was the armrest. I've owned patio sets where the armrest is so thin you can't set a drink on it. So the ad was about exactly that: an AI presenter sitting down and setting a drink right on the armrest. That's the moment a shopper pictures themselves in the chair.

Here's the four-beat structure I use:

  1. Hook (0–3 sec): a line or visual that makes someone stop. "Aluminum frame, never rusts" is a line from one of those patio ads.
  2. Product moment (3–8 sec): the real product, clearly visible, being used.
  3. The feature (8–12 sec): one benefit, shown, not just said.
  4. CTA (last 2–3 sec): where to go. "Link below" is fine.

Write it as a demo, not a testimonial. The presenter can show the product, describe what it does, and react to it. They should not claim to be a customer. That means no "I bought this last year," no "I've had it for six months," no "it changed my skin." Those lines describe an experience the AI person never had. More on why in the rules section below.

Step 3: Give the model the real product

This is the step that keeps your ad from turning into AI slop. The model needs two inputs before anything else.

A clean product photo. For the patio brand, all I had to start with was messy outdoor photos of the set. I cleaned the product onto a white background, so the model knew exactly what it was supposed to show. If you don't have a clean shot yet, my Shopify product photo guide walks through how to take one with your phone.

A brand kit. Your logo, colors, fonts and a few sample headlines, in one reference image. That way, when the model adds an end card or text, it uses your brand and doesn't make one up.

It's critical to have both. Without them, the model will happily "improve" your product, and now the ad shows something you don't sell.

Step 4: Prompt the video, including what NOT to do

I make these with Seedance 2.5 inside Higgsfield. The same structure works in other video tools.

The thing most people miss: they tell the model what they want, and never what they don't want. I always add a Negative section.

Here's a starting template you can copy. Swap in your product and your script:

Selfie-style vertical video (9:16), handheld phone feel.
A [age range] [person description] sits on the [product] from the
reference image in a [setting] at [time of day].
They look into the camera and say: "[your script, one or two short lines]."
Close-up as they [the one feature in action, e.g. set a drink on the armrest].
Natural daylight, light ambient sound, no music.
Use the reference image for the product exactly as shown.

Negative: do not change the product's shape, color, material or logo.
No extra products. No legible text other than the brand logo.
No distorted hands or faces. No people in the background.
No claims of owning or using the product before.

That last negative line is there on purpose. It keeps the model from improvising a fake review on you.

Step 5: Generate, rerun, and check the product

You're going to have to rerun a few of these and tweak each prompt. That's normal. I usually generate a handful of takes and keep the best one.

Before anything goes live, check each take against your clean product photo:

  • Product: same shape, color and parts? Is the logo right?
  • Hands: right number of fingers, nothing melting into the product?
  • Words: does the voice say exactly your script, and nothing extra?
  • Claims: any price, result or "I've been using this" line you didn't write? Cut it.

On a test for a different patio set, the AI character ran a "would you sit here for $100?" challenge. The set wasn't $100. That works as a hook idea, but it's the kind of line you fix before an ad runs. A wrong price in a real ad is a real problem.

The line you can't cross: fake reviews

Rules as of October 2026. This is not legal advice.

In 2024 the FTC finalized a rule on fake reviews and testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (opens in a new tab). It says a business can't create or share a consumer testimonial that misrepresents:

  1. that the person exists,
  2. that the person actually used the product, or
  3. what their experience with it was.

An AI person saying "I've had this on my patio all summer" hits all three. They don't exist, they never used it, and the experience is made up.

An AI presenter showing the product, saying what it's made of and setting a drink on the armrest is a different thing. It's a demo, like a spokesperson in a commercial.

So here's what I'd do:

  • Script demos, not stories. Present tense, about the product, not about "my experience."
  • Say it's AI where people will see it, like an on-screen "AI-generated" note on the video and in your caption.
  • Never post AI people as reviews. Not on your product page, not in a review widget, not as a "customer" account.
  • Only claim what's true. Price, materials, delivery times, results. If it's in the script, you should be able to prove it.

To be quite honest, the curiosity factor is part of why these ads get clicks. People stop to figure out if it's real. That's fine for a hook. It's not fine for a review.

Common questions

Is AI UGC legal?

Making a video with an AI presenter isn't the problem. Making it look like a real customer's review is. The FTC's 2024 rule bans testimonials that misrepresent that the person exists or used the product. Keep it a demo, label it as AI, and check with a lawyer if you're unsure.

Do AI UGC ads actually work?

They can. The patio ad with the drink on the armrest had an amazing click-through rate for my client. But one good ad isn't proof for your product. Test a few against your current ads and let the numbers decide.

Should I use AI UGC instead of real creators?

They do different jobs. Real customers can give real reviews, which AI can't. AI is great for testing lots of hooks fast and for product demos you'd never pay a creator to film. Many brands will want both.

What do I need to start?

A clean photo of your product, a simple brand kit, a short script built around one feature, and an AI video tool. I use Seedance 2.5 in Higgsfield. You can make your first one this afternoon.
