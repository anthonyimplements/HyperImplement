How to make AI product photos for your Shopify store

The photo carries the product. The prompt carries the room. Here is the loop I use at HyperImplement so a Shopify catalog still looks like the thing you ship.

I run HyperImplement in Chicago. Most weeks I make AI product photos, ads, and UGC for brands that sell physical products, usually on Shopify. The photo tool I use is Higgsfield. This is the process, written so you can run it on your own catalog. If you would rather see it on your product first, claim 3 free product photos and I will make them from your store.

You do not need a studio. You do need a photo of the real product, a style you can repeat, and the habit of throwing out images that do not match what you ship.

Photograph the real product

AI is good at rooms. It is unreliable at inventing your product. Describe a bottle in a prompt and skip the photo, and you get a bottle. It will not be yours. The label shifts, the cap changes, the color drifts. I start every job from photos of the item I can hold.

A phone is enough. Stand next to a window. Turn the product toward the glass, not toward the sun. Direct sun gives you hard shadows and a blown-out label. A white poster board or a plain wall behind the product is better than a kitchen counter. You are not making the final photo. You are giving the model a clean look at the object.

What to shoot

  • Front, straight on, product filling most of the frame.
  • A three-quarter angle, the way it sits on a shelf.
  • The side, if the side carries information or a different shape.
  • One tight detail: the cap, the stitch, the texture, the part a buyer zooms into.
  • The top, if people need the opening, the lid, or a pattern.

One product per frame. If two products are in the shot, the model sometimes merges them. Leave out hands, and leave out text you added in another app.

If the label has small type, take one extra frame where that type is sharp. Keep it in a folder called "check" and do not upload it to the store. It is the answer key you compare against later.

More than one angle helps. Higgsfield's product photography tool is built to start from your photo, and a second angle gives it more of the shape. I still generate from one hero reference at a time, so I know which photo the result is supposed to match.

Prompt the scene, not the product

A weak prompt tries to redesign the product. "Luxury serum, premium, cinematic, 8k" invites a new bottle. A useful prompt describes only what is around the product you already photographed.

This is the paragraph I reuse. Fill the brackets per brand, then leave them alone.

Same product as the reference photo. Keep the shape, color, label, and cap unchanged.
Setting: [pale stone counter / white sweep / light oak table].
Light: soft daylight from the left, short shadow to the right.
Camera: straight on, product filling about 70% of the frame, no wide-angle stretch.
No extra products, no hands, no added logos, no text outside the product.

What to generate, and what to throw away

For a Shopify listing I usually want four frames, in this order:

  1. A clean studio or sweep shot. This becomes the first image.
  2. The same product on a simple surface. This is the second image.
  3. A detail crop of the part people zoom into.
  4. One wider scene, only if the product still reads at a glance.

Generate a few options. I look at four and keep one. The keep test is boring on purpose:

  • Could I pick this object out of a lineup of the real product?
  • Are the words on the label still the same words, or letter-shaped texture?
  • Did a handle, strap, or button appear that the real product does not have?
  • Would a customer recognize the color when the package arrives?

If any answer is no, delete it. Another generation is cheaper than a return.

In Higgsfield I upload the photo, pick a studio, lifestyle, or detail shot, and describe the scene. The menus move, so I am not going to give you a click path that will be stale next month. The check does not move: the file you upload has to be the product you sell. Their own page says the same thing in their words. Start from the real photo so shape, label, and color have something to follow, then check the result before you list it.

When a prompt starts wandering, I paste the paragraph above back in. For a long list of SKUs I keep that paragraph in a text file and use Claude to copy it with only the product name swapped. The photo still does the real work. The file stops the wording from drifting.

One look across the catalog

One strong image and eleven unrelated ones looks like a flea market. Collection pages show your first image side by side. If every SKU has a different table, a different time of day, and a different crop, the grid looks accidental.

Write a style card. Mine is one short paragraph, and I paste it into every prompt for that brand:

Surface: light oak, empty. Light: soft, slightly warm, from the left.
Shadow: short and soft. Crop: square, product centered, similar scale on every SKU.
Background: nothing else in the frame. Mood: calm daylight, no colored gels.

Change the reference photo for each product. Do not change the paragraph. If a dark colorway disappears on oak, change one word and note it on the card. That is the whole system.

Use the same aspect ratio on every featured image. Shopify's product media docs say collection grids line up when the main images share an aspect ratio. I use square.

Upload them to Shopify

Yes, you can put AI photos on a Shopify product page. Shopify does not have a separate rule for how the photo was made. The rule that matters is yours: the picture has to match the product you ship.

From those same docs: PNG is the file type they call best for most product images, then JPEG. They also accept WebP, GIF, HEIC, TIFF, BMP, and SVG. A product image has to be under 20 MB, and no larger than 5000 by 5000 pixels or 25 megapixels. For square images, 2048 by 2048 usually displays best. Shopify builds the smaller sizes your theme asks for, so upload a clean 2048 and let that happen. I would not upload a tiny file and hope the theme makes it sharp.

I export photographs as JPEG. I use PNG when I actually need a transparent background. Bigger than 2048 rarely helps the storefront, and it makes the upload slower.

Names, order, and alt text

Name the file before you export: brand-sku-01-front.jpg. On Shopify's file upload page, a name that ends in thumb, icon, small, compact, medium, large, grande, pico, or testing can be misread as a resize request. I keep the angle word in the middle of the name, not on the end, when I am unsure. acme-bottle-01-front.jpg is a safe pattern. acme-bottle-large.jpg is the one to avoid.

Order on the product:

  1. The clear shot. This is the featured image collection pages use. A shopper should know what is for sale in under a second.
  2. The three-quarter or the side.
  3. The detail.
  4. Lifestyle, after the facts.

Alt text is what Shopify shows if the image fails, and what a screen reader speaks. Their alt text guide says keep it brief. Around 125 characters is the recommendation. 512 is the maximum. Write what is in the photo, including the product name:

Matte black 16 oz bottle on a light oak table, front view.

Skip "image of." Skip a list of keywords. If the scene is a lifestyle frame, say so in the same plain way.

Before you save, open the file at full size next to the reference photo. Check the label, the color, and the edges. Then open the product page on your phone. If the first image is a wide scene and the product is a speck, swap it.

Mistakes that make a listing look fake

  1. Prompting a product you did not photograph. The result is a category, not your SKU.
  2. Publishing label text you did not read. The model often keeps the shape of a word and changes the spelling. If you sell supplements, cosmetics, or anything with a claim on the package, read the label at full size.
  3. A different hero style on every product. The grid is the brand. The single image is not.
  4. A scene that hides the product. A dark background is useless if I cannot tell what is for sale.
  5. Color you did not check, on a product where color is the purchase. Lipstick, paint, fabric, and styled food drift. If the buyer is choosing a shade, use a real photo for that frame.

When AI beats a shoot, and when it does not

I use both. The choice is about the product, not about being current. A traditional shoot and an AI set are different jobs. Sometimes the shoot is the point. Sometimes you have forty SKUs to update and a studio is not on the calendar. I am not going to invent a savings number for you.

How I choose
Situation What I do
A lot of SKUs, and the product is a rigid object: bottle, box, tool, packaged good AI, from a real photo of each one
You want to test a holiday table, a bathroom shelf, or a clean sweep before you book anything AI, then keep the scene that belongs on the page
The same item needs a listing photo and a few ad frames One reference photo, several scenes, same style card
Color has to match a swatch: dye lots, lipstick, paint A real photo for that frame
The buyer will zoom in on craft: jewelry, stitching, a glass edge A real photo, or a very tight reference and a hard check
A person has to wear it, hold it, or show scale, and the fit is the product A real photo
The package has legal text that has to be exact A real photo, unless you read every word on the AI frame
You do not have a physical sample Do not list an image the model invented

A split I use often: real photos for the plain "what is in the box" frames, and AI for the lifestyle frames built from those same photos. The listing stays honest. The grid still has some life. You can see the kind of still and moving work on the homepage. The studio page is HyperImplement.

Run it in an afternoon

Ten products, one window, one style card.

  1. Clear a table by a window. Use a white board or a plain wall.
  2. Photograph each product: front, three-quarter, detail. One product in the frame.
  3. Write the style card. Square crop. Name the surface and the light.
  4. Upload the front photo. Ask for a clean sweep, paste the style card, generate a few options, and keep the one that still matches. Repeat for one lifestyle frame.
  5. Export square, 2048 pixels on each side, JPEG for photos. Name them brand-sku-01-front.jpg.
  6. Upload to the Shopify product. First image is the clear shot. Write alt text in plain language.
  7. On your phone, open the collection. If one thumbnail looks like it came from a different store, fix that product before you touch the next one.

That is the loop. The slow part is the check. The check is what keeps "this does not look like what arrived" out of your reviews.
